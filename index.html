Equation Automata

A traffic cellular automaton whose coupling rule is built from the condition p(1−p) = 1/8 — an equation that turns out to be the self-dual point of the 2D Ising model in disguise.

01

Magnetism in traffic

Driving is a balance of two forces. Every car is pulled forward to take the position of the car ahead, and pushed back to keep a safe distance. Attraction and repulsion between neighbours is exactly the vocabulary of magnetism, so the project asks a simple question: if traffic is written as a lattice of interacting cells, does the physics of the Ising model show up?

Each cell of an \(L\times L\) torus is either a car (1) or empty road (0). The grid starts random with car probability \(p\), and three local rules move cars forward, resolve lane conflicts and couple each car to its neighbourhood.

03

Three rules

The automaton is deliberately small. There are no trigonometric functions anywhere in the code; every constant below comes out of counting neighbours.

RULE 1

Inverse cell update

Instead of a cell reading its neighbours, a car writes to them. Its own state dictates the next state of its neighbourhood, which produces forward driving motion along one axis.

RULE 2

Moore → von Neumann shift

Cars compare counts in the 8-cell Moore neighbourhood with the 4-cell von Neumann cross. The competition between the two models lane conflicts.

RULE 3

Critical coupling

A car with \(g\) Moore neighbours moves right if \(g/8 > p(1-p)\) and left if \(g/8 < p(1-p)\), tying the motion to the initial density.

Where the critical equation comes from

Setting the coupling to its smallest non-trivial value, a single neighbour (\(g=1\)), gives a quadratic whose roots are the first constants of the project:

$$\frac{g}{8} = p(1-p)\;\xrightarrow{\;g=1\;}\; p^2-p+\tfrac18=0 \;\Longrightarrow\; p=\tfrac12\pm\tfrac{1}{2\sqrt2}=\cos^2\!\tfrac{\pi}{8},\ \sin^2\!\tfrac{\pi}{8}$$
04

The Kramers–Wannier connection

The 2D square-lattice Ising model has its critical point where it is self-dual, \(\sinh 2K_c = 1\), i.e. \(K_c = \tfrac12\ln(1+\sqrt2)\). The roots of the coupling equation are not merely close to Ising quantities — they are exactly the same condition written differently.

$$p(1-p)=\tfrac18 \iff (2p-1)^2=\tfrac12 \iff \tanh 2K=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\quad\text{with}\quad 2p-1=\tanh 2K$$

Under that identification the upper root is the heat-bath probability of a spin aligning with two neighbours at criticality, \(\cos^2(\pi/8) = \sigma(4K_c) = 1/(1+e^{-4K_c})\). The remaining identities follow:

\(\tan\tfrac{\pi}{8}=\sqrt2-1=\tanh K_c=e^{-2K_c}\)self-duality
\(\operatorname{gd}(2K_c)=\tfrac{\pi}{4}\)Gudermannian bridge, tan ↔ tanh
\(\cos^2\tfrac{\pi}{8}-\sin^2\tfrac{\pi}{8}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}=\tanh 2K_c\)difference of the two roots
\(\tfrac12+\tfrac12\tanh K_c=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\)1D chain conditional probability at Kc
\(\langle\sigma_{0,0}\sigma_{0,1}\rangle_{T_c}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\)nearest-neighbour correlation (McCoy–Wu)
\(1-e^{-2K_c}=2-\sqrt2\)Swendsen–Wang bond probability at Tc
Why it matters. The Moore neighbourhood has 8 cells, and \(4p(1-p)=\tfrac12\) is the same statement as \(\sin^2(\pi/4)=\tfrac12\). That single fact links a neighbour-counting rule to the self-dual angle \(\pi/8\). The open question is whether the automaton's dynamics earn the identification \(2p-1=\tanh 2K\), or whether it is only built into the rule.
05

What the simulation shows

The "tangent graph" is a function of density

The sum of all Moore counts is exactly eight times the number of cars, \(j = 8\cdot\text{count}_1\), because every car is seen by its eight neighbours. The plotted quantity therefore reduces to the car density \(\rho\):

$$\frac{1}{\text{ratio}_1}=\frac{\text{count}_0(\text{count}_0+\text{count}_1)}{8\,\text{count}_1^2}=\frac{1-\rho}{8\rho^2}$$

Its four branches — about 0.48, 0.51, 1.01 and 2.25 — are the settled densities of the two rule regimes described next.

Two rule regimes, switching at the roots

Because \(g\) is an integer, the comparison \(g/8\) vs \(p(1-p)\) only changes when \(p(1-p)\) crosses \(1/8\), which happens exactly at \(\cos^2(\pi/8)\) and \(\sin^2(\pi/8)\). Between the roots a single neighbour sends a car left; outside them it sends it right. Moving \(p\) by \(10^{-9}\) across the root changes the settled density discontinuously:

Inside the roots every value of \(p\) gives the same rule, so the curves for 0.1464, 0.4407, 0.6478 and 0.8536 differ only through their starting density.

The self-consistent density

The most interesting number is the density \(p^*\) that the automaton maps back onto itself: start at \(p^*\) and it settles at \(p^*\). With the critical thresholds (6/6) it lands next to the Onsager value:

Status. At \(L=800\) with long runs \(p^*\) matches \(K_c\) to four digits, but it still drifts with lattice size and run length. Whether it converges to \(K_c\) as \(L\to\infty\) is the central open measurement. The long-run state is nearly frozen (a near period-4 cycle), so standard critical signatures — growing fluctuations, power-law correlations — have not been observed yet.
06

Extending to 3D

In three dimensions the Moore neighbourhood has 26 neighbours inside a 27-cell cube, and a direction plane has \(3\times3=9\) cells. The same construction gives a new coupling equation with clean rational roots:

$$\frac{g}{27} > p(1-p),\qquad p^2-p+\tfrac{6}{27}=0\;\Longrightarrow\; p=\tfrac13,\ \tfrac23$$

07

Open questions

  1. Does \(p^*\) converge to \(K_c\)? Finite-size and finite-time scaling up to \(L\approx 3200\), with runs long enough that the density stops drifting.
  2. Is \(p^*\) robust? Repeat with synchronous or randomised update order and small rule perturbations. A value that survives is a property of the rules, not of the scan direction.
  3. Can it be derived? A mean-field treatment of the three rules gives the expected next density as a polynomial in \(p\); its fixed point would explain the number analytically.
  4. Add a real temperature. Let rules fire with a Glauber probability set by \(\beta\) and look for Binder-cumulant crossings and Ising exponents (\(\beta=1/8,\ \nu=1\)).
  5. 3D from simulation. Measure the 3D self-consistent density and compare it with \(K_c^{(3D)}\approx 0.22165\).
  6. Traffic. Position the model against Biham–Middleton–Levine, Rule 184 and Nagel–Schreckenberg, and test the jamming/synchronisation picture against real flow and accident data.
